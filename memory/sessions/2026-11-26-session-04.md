### Ordinary lesson checks

1. **Prompt:** “For that internal IT agent, how would you describe its job, audience, and pain point? One short sentence or three bullets is enough.” Context: tutor example internal IT agent helps nontechnical employees troubleshoot sign-in, not automatically administer accounts.

   **Initial learner response:** “The job would be "support with login issues" audience woul dbe "users", and pain-points would be sign-in issues and permissions”

   **Feedback:** Valid login/access scope, but users too broad. Tutor narrowed the audience to nontechnical employees using company applications and distinguished explaining an access error from granting access. Tutor-refined specification: help nontechnical employees troubleshoot company-app sign-in problems and identify access issues needing IT escalation. No independent audience-refinement response.

2. **Prompt:** “Suppose the agent can search IT help articles but has no account-management tools. Would you offer the display prompt ‘Unlock my account’? Why or why not—and what would you offer instead?”

   **Initial learner response:** “No, I would have to give prompts that will help the user search for capabilities like help articles and other tools.”

   **Feedback:** Correctly rejects an unsupported action promise; user-facing prompts should reflect the user's goal rather than tool names. Tutor-supplied alternative: “How can I recover access to my account?” The goal-based replacement was not independently supplied.

3. **Prompt:** “Give two test requests for this agent: one ordinary sign-in question and one permissions-related boundary case. What should a good response do in each?”

   **Initial learner response:** “smoke tes for ordinary sign in; and automated evaluations for boundary cases”

   **Feedback:** This names testing approaches, not requests. Smoke tests can include boundary cases and larger evaluations can include ordinary cases; the distinction is mainly size and speed. Tutor worked ordinary request: “I forgot my work-account password. What should I do?” Expected: approved guidance grounded in articles, with no false claim of reset.

4. **Scaffolded follow-up prompt:** “Your turn—just the boundary case: A user asks, ‘Give me administrator access so I can install this application.’ Our agent can only search help articles. What should its response do, and what must it not claim to have done?”

   **Learner response:** “There should be some guardails that is applied with the models, which kinda prevents prompts from doing more than what they are supposed to do.”

   **Feedback:** Relevant general scope-control idea, but not a concrete response. Tutor supplied the worked answer: explain inability to grant access, provide the approved request process or IT escalation, and never claim permissions changed. Tutor distinguished behavioral guidance from tools/permissions and noted model guardrails must not be the sole privileged-action protection.

5. **Confirmation prompt:** “If the agent replies, ‘Done—you now have administrator access,’ but no permissions actually changed, is that acceptable because the technical boundary held? Why or why not?”

   **Learner response:** “No. It hsould have responded with an error of what has not happend and why.”

   **Feedback:** Independently rejects false success and requires explaining what did not happen; refine error wording to a useful limitation plus next step. Tutor example: “I can’t grant administrator access. Please follow your organization’s access-request process or contact IT.” This is scaffolded recovery after the tutor-supplied boundary answer, not independent construction of rubric or test cases.
